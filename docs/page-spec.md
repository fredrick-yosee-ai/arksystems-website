## 0. What changed from v1, and why

v1 was well written, but it told the visitor four separate times what ArkSystems had not yet done. That is not honesty — it is bad copy that costs money. v2 keeps every truthfulness constraint and removes every confession.

The shift in three lines:

- **Sell a product, not a practice.** A named thing with a clear shape, a known timescale and a visible end. Categories don't convert.
- **Prove capability by demonstration, specificity and risk reversal** rather than by borrowed credibility. These are the proof types available today, and they convert as hard as testimonials.
- **One action, everywhere.** Book the call that contains the demo.

The page makes no claim that requires a disclaimer, so it carries no disclaimer.

## 1a. The reader-psychology standard

*This governs every client-facing asset, not just this page. The full version lives in `05 Brand Guide`.*

**1. Value before cost, always.** A person who meets a price before the value has landed stops evaluating the offer and starts assembling reasons to decline. That switch does not reverse later in the same visit. Nothing commercial appears until the reader already wants the outcome.

**2. Write their relief, not our deliverable.** "Here's what it would take and what it costs" describes our output. "We can take that off you" describes their week. Same fact, opposite effect.

**3. Let them feel the after-state before anything is asked of them.** "What it looks like when that job stops being yours." "Month-end just arrives." The absence of the task has to be imaginable before a request is made.

**4. Money language is allowed when it protects the reader.** "We'd rather tell you not to buy something than sell you something that doesn't pay for itself" mentions money and *lowers* defences, because it is spending our interest rather than asking for theirs. The test is direction: language that protects them builds trust, language that asks of them raises guard.

**5. Reduce the cost of the first step to almost nothing.** Twenty minutes, no pitch, no slides, and worth it even if nothing follows.

**6. Recognition must be paid off with relief.** Pain sections make a reader agree; only relief makes them act. A page that diagnoses more than it relieves is admired and abandoned.

**7. Write for a reader whose first language may not be English.** Metro Vancouver's owner-operators are a genuinely diverse group, and a large share of them run their businesses in a second language. The risk is not technical words — those are easy to spot. It is idiom: *off your plate, no meter running, earns its keep, a sit-down, in the books, the shoebox.* Every one is invisible to a native speaker and opaque to everyone else.

Use short sentences, common words, and concrete nouns. Avoid metaphors that depend on North American culture. Prefer "we would" to "we'd" where the contraction crowds the sentence. Simple is not flat — "No more Saturdays sorting out receipts" is plainer *and* more vivid than "No Saturday with the shoebox", because a real thing replaced a figure of speech.

**8. Blame the work, never the owner.** A reader who feels judged stops evaluating the offer and starts defending their business — the same shutdown as a premature price, from the other direction. The pain on the page is always the fault of the work, the volume, or the way information arrives — never of how the reader runs things. Where possible, make the pain a symptom of success: "the busier you get, the more of this there is" lets a reader admit the problem without admitting a failure. Flattery that is true is allowed and works — "someone whose time is worth far more than this" names the same problem as "the wrong person is doing this" and lands as a compliment. The offer is framed as optimization — making a good business faster and giving hours back to revenue — not as rescue. Words that never describe the reader's business: weak, broken, mess, chaos, behind, failing, outdated, inefficient.

**9. Open and close on gain.** Headlines are what a skimming reader actually reads, so the emotional arc of the page lives in them. Pain may appear in body copy where it does recognition work, but the first headline and the last one always name the gain — find the value, get the hours back. A page that opens on a problem and closes on an annoyance leaves the reader heavier than it found them, however good the middle was.

## 1b. The AI positioning rule

*Added Aug 21, 2026. This is why ArkSystems gets a conversation at all, and it belongs in `05 Brand Guide` alongside the psychology standard.*

**The market condition.** Every business owner has been told they need AI. Almost none of them can say which part of their week it should touch. They are reading about it, being sold it, and in many cases already experimenting with it — while the work that actually slows their week down stays manual and hard to keep track of.

**The gap is the market.** ArkSystems does not sell AI. AI is everywhere and nobody needs help buying it. ArkSystems sells the answer to *where* — which job, worth how much, and whether it is worth doing at all.

**What this means for copy.**

- **Say AI.** It is the reason a reader stops. Removing it does not make the writing more honest, it makes ArkSystems harder to place. A page that never says AI reads as admin help, and competes with virtual assistants instead of occupying the category.
- **Name the confusion, not the technology.** The reader's problem is not that AI is unavailable. It is that they cannot tell where it would be worth using. That sentence is the whole positioning.
- **AI names the category and the confusion — never the promise.** "Where AI is worth using" is allowed. "AI will transform your business", "AI-powered growth", "the future of work" are not, and remain on the avoid list.
- **AI is one of the few technical words that survives the plain-language rule.** It is understood across languages and by non-technical readers, which is exactly why it belongs and why the words around it must stay simple.

**The balance to hold.** AI earns attention; the specific job earns belief; the relief earns the booking. A page that only says AI is hype. A page that never says AI is invisible. Sections 1 and 2 carry AI, section 3 shows it doing one real thing, and everything after that is about their week.

## 2. Homepage

### Section 1 — Hero

**Purpose:** Name their problem in one line, show the machine working, and offer one action.

**Eyebrow:**
Your AI Partner for Smarter Growth

**H1:**
Find where AI can create real value in your business.

**Subhead:**
Everyone is talking about AI. Very few people can tell you which part of your week it should touch first. We find the one job that would give you back the most time, and take it off you. Usually in two or three weeks.

*Why this H1.* It names the category you are in and the job you actually do, in one line a reader can repeat. The previous H1 — "The admin work is quietly running your week" — was more visceral but never said the word AI, which left the page reading as admin help rather than as the AI partner. That line is not lost: it moves into section 2, where the pain belongs.

**Line under the CTA:**
On the call we'll show you one of these running, and tell you honestly whether it's worth doing for you.

**Micro-trust line:**
Practical · Measurable · Business-first

**Two changes to the widget:**

### Section 2 — The problem, made concrete

**Purpose:** Let them recognise themselves. Four symptoms, no industry named.

**H2:**
The real problem is not a lack of AI tools.

**Line under the H2:**
It is not knowing where they would actually be worth using.

**Body:**
Plenty of businesses are already trying AI somewhere. Meanwhile the work that actually slows the week down stays slow, manual and hard to keep track of.

None of this means anything is wrong with how you run your business. It is what happens when a business does well — the busier you get, the more of this there is. And the problem usually is not the work itself. It is everything around the work. Most businesses don't need better technology. They need fewer things being passed between people. Information comes in five different ways. Someone has to ask for it. Someone has to type it in. And nobody can see what is waiting unless they ask.

It never looks urgent. Whether it falls to the owner or is shared across a team of twelve, it quietly takes hours that should go into the work you get paid for.

**Four cards:**

**Work keeps waiting on information**
Missing documents, unclear handoffs, and repeated follow-up stop things from moving.

**The admin finds your busiest people**
Receipts, invoices, following up and typing things in end up with whoever gets things done — often the owner, often someone whose time is worth far more than this, often both.

**Nobody can see what's stuck**
The answer is in someone's email, or a spreadsheet, or only in their head. To find out what is stuck, you have to ask.

**AI feels relevant but unclear**
You know it matters. You don't know which use is worth the money, or where to start.

**Sizing note — added Aug 21.** v1 opened this section with "most owner-led businesses" and framed the admin card entirely around the owner. That made the whole page read as if it were for sole operators, when the target spans businesses from roughly CAD $500,000 to $5 million in revenue, many of which have staff. **Section 2 is the universal problem and must stay size-agnostic. Section 5 is the only place the two doors are distinguished.** Do not reintroduce segmentation above section 5.

### Section 3 — See it work

**Purpose:** The highest-value block on the page, and the emotional centre of it. Everything before this makes the reader agree something is wrong. This is where they feel what it is like when it stops.

**Give it the most room on the page.** Full-bleed olive band, the tallest section, panels at real size, generous space around them. Pain works in tight copy; relief does not. A cramped payoff reads as a footnote, and this is the payoff every earlier section has been setting up.

**H2:**
A receipt goes in. A clean line item comes out. You approve everything in between.

**Body:**
This is an AI system we built to handle receipts, shown here with sample data. You send the receipt the way you would send it anyway. Everything after that stops being your problem.

**Three panels**, redrawn from real screenshots in brand tokens as HTML/SVG, matching the hero widget's visual language. **Labels are written from the reader's side, not the machine's** — the same three steps described as things that happen to them:

1. **You send it and forget it**
   *photographed on your phone, 7:42pm*
2. **It reads it. You just check it.**
   *Westside Supply Co. · $248.60 · suggested: office supplies*
3. **It's already in your accounts**
   *clean line item, ready for month-end*

*"You just check it" is the review step reframed. In v1 it read as an obligation the reader still carries; here it reads as the last small thing left, which is what it actually is. Keep the panel visually highlighted — it is still the trust anchor.*

**What changes — set after the panels, three short lines, no paragraph:**

No more Saturdays sorting out receipts.
Month-end takes an hour, not a whole evening.
Nobody emails you asking where a receipt went.

*This beat is what rebalances the page. Without it the section describes what the thing does; with it, the section describes what their month looks like afterwards. Roughly fifteen words, and it converts a demonstration into a resolution.*

**Caption, visible under the panels:**
Illustration using sample data.

*v1's caption read "Illustration using sample data. No client data is used." The second sentence is cut — it raises clients unprompted, and the shorter line says everything true that the longer one did.*

**CTA:** See it working — book 20 minutes

**Later:** the 90-second video replaces the panels in the same slot with no redesign. The "what changes" lines stay either way — they are not tied to the format of the demonstration.

**Not to be done:** do not move this section above section 2. Showing the fix before the problem is felt turns it into a product demo instead of a resolution, and the recognition work in section 2 is what gives it its charge.

### Section 5 — What happens when you book

**Why this section exists.** Everything before it makes the visitor recognise their problem. This is the only block that tells them what the one action on the page actually involves — and not knowing that is the biggest single reason a warm visitor doesn't book. It absorbs the old commercial section, because "what happens if I say yes" and "what am I risking" are the same conversation.

**H2:**
What happens when you book.

**Intro:**
Twenty minutes. No sales pitch, no slides.

**Step 1 — The call**
You bring the job that annoys you most. We'll show you one of these running, so you can see for yourself what it looks like when that job stops being yours.

**Step 2 — What you'll hear back**
Either: yes, we can take that off you, and here's what that would look like. Or: this one is more complicated than it looks, and it is worth spending a day on it properly before anyone builds anything. Or: you don't actually need us — here's the simpler thing to do instead. That last one happens more often than you'd expect.

**Closing line:**
Even if we do nothing together, you will know exactly where your week is going. That is worth twenty minutes on its own.

**CTA:** See it working — book 20 minutes

**Build notes:**

- **The workshop lives in step 2, as a clause.** "Worth spending a proper day on it first" is the whole mention — no name, no price, no button. It becomes something recommended on the call, once there is enough context to justify it, which is how it actually sells.
- **No commercial terms in this section — not one word.** A third step covering price, staged payment and ownership was written and cut, and a cost reference inside step 2 was cut after it. Both planted "this is going to cost money" at the moment someone was deciding whether to spend twenty free minutes. Naming a cost before the value has landed switches the reader from evaluating the offer to assembling reasons to decline, and that shift does not reverse later in the same visit. The visitor's risk at this stage is wasting an afternoon, not overpaying.
- **Every outcome is written as their relief, not our deliverable.** "Here's what it would take and what it costs" is a description of our output. "We can take that off you" is a description of their week. Same fact, opposite effect.
- **"When that job stops being yours"** is the after-state stated plainly. The visitor should be able to feel the absence of the task before they are asked for anything.
- **Risk reversal moves to the FAQ**, where "what does this cost", "what if it doesn't do what we agreed" and "who owns it" already carry it. Someone thinking about money goes looking for it; everyone else is left alone. It belongs on the workshop page and in the proposal too.
- **The section closes on value, not commitment.** "Even if we do nothing together, you will know exactly where your week is going" makes the call worth taking even if nothing follows, which removes the hesitation without ever mentioning price.
- **"The third one happens more often than you'd expect"** is the most valuable sentence in the section. It is the proof that the advice is honest, and it costs nothing to say.
- **The page carries curiosity and recognised pain, not commercial pressure.** Anything that reads as a step toward an invoice belongs after the conversation, not before it.

**What was removed, and why.** This slot previously held two cards routing to two purchasable offers, then two cards routing to the same call. The second version was redundant: sections 2 and 4 already do symptom recognition, so a third recognition block read as padding no matter how it was worded. Redundancy is a structural problem and no rewrite fixes it.

**Language rule for this section — and the page.** Describe the problem in the reader's words and the fix in the reader's words. The customer does not have a workflow, they have a drawer full of receipts. Words that stay off the page: workflow, agent, human-in-the-loop, review step, fixed scope, integration, implementation, solution, system of record. The mechanism is our concern, not theirs — they need to know the job goes away and that nothing happens without their say-so.

### Section 9 — FAQ, rebuilt as objection handling

**Purpose changed completely.** An FAQ is where you close, not where you disclose. Every answer removes a reason not to book. Buying questions come first.

**How long does it take?**
Most jobs take two to three weeks from go-ahead to handing it back working. If something would take longer, you'll hear that before you commit, not halfway through.

**What if it doesn't do what we agreed?**
What we're building is written down before we start, and you pay in stages against it. Nothing's final until you've used it and told us it works.

**What does this cost?**
Every job has a set price, agreed before anything starts. The price does not go up as we go. We work it out after a short conversation, because it depends on what the job involves. The workshop has its own price, on its own page.

**Do I have to change the software I already use?**
Usually not. The first thing we look for is whether something you already pay for can do the job, set up properly or connected to something else. Replacing what you use is expensive and disruptive, and it's rarely the cheapest answer.

**Who owns it when you're done?**
You do. It runs on your accounts and you keep the work. There's no ArkSystems subscription attached to it.

**What happens to my data?**
We only get access to what the job actually needs, and nothing more. Anything that matters still goes past a person before it happens — that's deliberate, not a setting. Full detail on the data handling page.

**Do you guarantee savings or ROI?**
No, and be careful of anyone who does. Financial estimates are built from your inputs and clearly stated assumptions, so you can check the arithmetic yourself. We make assumptions and missing information visible, and we don't promise results we can't control.

**Do you always recommend AI?**
No. The answer might be a clearer way of doing it, better use of something you already pay for, connecting two things you already use, automating a small piece of it, or leaving it alone for now. We use AI where it earns its place — not because it's fashionable.

**What kinds of work do you look at?**
Recurring work involving document collection, follow-up, approvals, status visibility, information handoffs, and repetitive administration.

**Removed:** "Have you done this before?" and its answer. It answered a question nobody asked, at the worst possible moment, and it was the single most expensive sentence on the page.

**Reinstated in v2.2:** "Do you guarantee savings or ROI?" was cut from v2 as a weakness. That was wrong. Answered this way it is a credibility play, not a confession — it separates ArkSystems from anyone promising a number, and "check the arithmetic yourself" is a stronger trust signal than silence. The live site already answered it well; this version sharpens it.

## 3. Workshop page (minimal, ships in release one)

A focused day on the job that is costing you the most.

For businesses where several things could run faster and it isn't obvious which to fix first. One day, one job, a clear answer at the end of it.

**What you leave with:**
A short written summary — what that job is costing you today on your own numbers, what your options are, and what we'd do next. Including "leave it for now" when that's the honest answer.

Not just a report. A clear path to getting it fixed.

**Investment:**
CAD $1,000, paid in advance.

The full $1,000 is credited toward an ArkSystems implementation sprint of $7,500 or more, when the agreement is signed and the deposit is paid within 30 days of your findings discussion. The credit is non-refundable, non-transferable, and excludes taxes, software subscriptions and third-party costs.

**Line under investment:**
The workshop stands on its own. Every estimate is built from your numbers and clearly stated assumptions, so you can check the arithmetic yourself.

**CTA:** Book a 20-minute conversation first

**Role of this page changed in v2.3.** It is no longer a homepage door. It is a reference page — reachable from the nav and footer, sent to people after a discovery call, and the page the accounting route needs. Nobody is expected to arrive here cold and buy.

**Two flags:**

- The credit terms necessarily disclose the **$7,500 sprint floor**. That's the first price signal on the site beyond the workshop itself. It has to be there for the terms to be honest, but you should know it's there.
- **No-guarantee wording** is handled positively on the page ("check the arithmetic yourself") and belongs in full on the Terms page rather than here.

Booking runs on **Cal.com**, not Calendly — switched Aug 24 2026. Event: *Ark Discovery Call*.

Two things follow from the platform choice:

- The event must be set to **20 minutes**. The page says "book 20 minutes" in five places and "Twenty minutes" twice more. If the event is any other length the page is making a claim that is not true, which the honesty rules do not allow.
- Cal.com's embed (`embed.js`) can open the booker in a popup instead of sending the visitor to another domain. Worth doing — every navigation away from the page is a chance to lose them — but it loads third-party script, so it has to appear in the Privacy page alongside analytics.

## 5. Honesty rules for production

Stated positively, since none of this appears on the page as text:

- No client names, testimonials, results, metrics, logos or "trusted by"
- Nothing states or implies a completed client engagement
- Illustrations are labelled as illustrations using sample data
- The page makes no market or industry claim, so it needs no source
- Off the site: AI transformation · revolutionary AI · replace your staff · fully autonomous agents · one-click automation · effortless transformation · guaranteed ROI · case study · our clients · results
- **Superseded:** v1's requirement that "This is a new offer with no completed client implementations yet" appear visibly on the page. Removed Aug 21. The page instead makes no claim that requires it — that trade is the reason removal is safe, and it holds only while the page stays free of client-implying language.
